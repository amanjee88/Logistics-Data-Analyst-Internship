This report presents an end-to-end predictive modeling and optimization exercise for a fictional e-commerce logistics operator. The objective is to estimate shipment delivery duration at dispatch time and demonstrate how forecasts can inform operational review and vehicle-selection decisions. The project is designed as a reproducible portfolio exercise; it does not use actual shipment records or claim performance improvements at a real company.

A deterministic synthetic dataset containing 1,200 shipment records was generated for the analysis. The predictors include delivery distance, shipment weight and volume, route stops, traffic index, weather severity, warehouse load, vehicle type, origin region, shipment priority, and day of week. Delivery time in hours is the target variable. A fixed random seed makes the data generation repeatable, while the report documents the assumptions used to create the hypothetical observations.

Three reference approaches were evaluated: a median-prediction baseline, Linear Regression, and a hyperparameter-tuned Random Forest regressor. The data were divided into 80% training and 20% holdout test sets. Five-fold cross-validation on the training portion was used to compare the baseline and predictive candidates, while a separate three-fold grid search was used to tune the Random Forest. Model selection was based on training cross-validation MAE before using the holdout set for final evaluation. Linear Regression had the lowest cross-validation MAE among the predictive candidates. On the untouched 240-row test set, it achieved a mean absolute error of approximately 0.826 hours, root mean squared error of approximately 1.262 hours, and R-squared of approximately 0.955. Because the outcome was generated from known synthetic relationships, these scores are demonstration results and should not be interpreted as expected accuracy on real logistics data.

The project includes actual-versus-predicted and residual diagnostics, model metric comparisons, Random Forest feature-importance diagnostics, and regional error analysis. It also demonstrates a capacity-constrained vehicle what-if heuristic using assumed vehicle capacities, estimated cost rates, and a stated time-value trade-off. On the test subset, the scenario changed the suggested vehicle for 82 of 240 shipments. The estimated cost and objective comparisons depend entirely on hypothetical rates and constraints and are not measured operational savings.

The accompanying Python code generates the dataset, preprocesses features in modeling pipelines, performs cross-validation and hyperparameter tuning, calculates MAE/RMSE/R-squared, creates charts and result files, and produces candidate vehicle recommendations. Limitations, feature leakage precautions, the difference between correlation and causation, and future requirements for chronological validation and real route-level constraints are discussed. All data, model outputs, cost estimates, and recommendations are labeled as synthetic or illustrative. The report concludes with practical next steps for validating any future model using appropriately governed real operational data before production use.
